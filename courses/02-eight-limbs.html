Part 0

Yoga's operating system

In Session 1 we learned that Patanjali systematized yoga. This session opens the system. The Yoga Sutras are a compact manual - a few hundred terse lines - that lay out how the mind works and how to still it. Their most famous contribution is ashtanga, the eight-limbed path (ashta = eight, anga = limb): a structured route from how you treat the world, through the body and breath, all the way to absorption. Learn these eight, in order, and you have the skeleton every other philosophy session hangs on.

The one thing to unlearn The poses (asana) are just one of the eight limbs - the third. Most of yoga's path is not physical at all. Holding that in mind changes how you will teach: a posture class is one-eighth of the system, not the whole of it.
Part 1 · covers the structure of the Yoga Sutras

The Yoga Sutras, structured 4 min live

Before the eight limbs, know the shape of the book they live in. The Yoga Sutras are organized into four padas (chapters), each with its own job. You do not need to memorize the sutra counts - but seeing the four-part arc tells you where the eight limbs sit and why the famous verses land where they do.

LiveThe four padas, one line each2 min▶

Four chapters, each a stage of the journey from "what is the problem?" to "here is freedom":

  • 1 · Samadhi Pada - the contemplation chapter. It defines yoga (this is where Sutra 1.2 lives), describes the vrittis (mental fluctuations), and maps the types of absorption. The "what and why".
  • 2 · Sadhana Pada - the practice chapter. Kriya yoga, the five kleshas (afflictions), and the first five limbs. The most practical chapter for a new teacher.
  • 3 · Vibhuti Pada - the powers chapter. The last three limbs (which combine into samyama) and the extraordinary abilities (siddhis) said to arise.
  • 4 · Kaivalya Pada - the liberation chapter. Kaivalya - freedom, aloneness of pure awareness - the ultimate aim.
Exam-honest note The Yoga Sutras are usually counted as 195 or 196 sutras depending on the manuscript and commentary - the counts above (51 / 55 / 56 / 34) sum to 196, the more common tally. If asked, give the range rather than insisting on one number. A well-read teacher knows the sources genuinely differ.
Real world

Why the structure helps you as a teacher. When a curious student asks "where does that quote come from?", you can place it instantly - "that's from the first chapter, where Patanjali defines the whole practice." Knowing the four-part shape means you never have to bluff about the text you are teaching from.

Self-studyWhat "sutra" actually means1 min read▶

The word sutra means "thread" - the same root as "suture". Each sutra is a single tightly compressed line, meant to be memorized and then unpacked by a teacher. That is why they feel so terse: they were never meant to stand alone. They are threads a living teacher weaves into understanding, which is exactly what a commentary - or a course like this - does.

Part 2 · covers ashtanga, the eight-limbed path

Ashtanga: the eight limbs 6 min live

Here is the heart of the session. The eight limbs (introduced in Sutra 2.29) are a ladder: you begin with how you meet the outer world and climb inward toward absorption. Notice the arc - the first two limbs are external and ethical, the last three are internal and absorptive, with the body and breath in between as the bridge.

LiveThe outer limbs: yama & niyama (1-2)2 min▶

The path does not start with the body - it starts with how you behave. The first two limbs are the ethical foundation, and the whole rest of the ladder is unstable without them.

  • Yama (1) - five ethical restraints: how you relate to the world around you (non-harming, truthfulness, and more - full list in Part 3).
  • Niyama (2) - five personal observances: how you relate to yourself (cleanliness, contentment, discipline, self-study, surrender).

Together they are yoga's ethical code. Patanjali puts them first on purpose: a calm mind cannot be built on a life of harm and dishonesty. Character before posture.

LiveThe body & breath limbs: asana, pranayama, pratyahara (3-5)2 min▶

These three are the bridge between the outer ethical limbs and the inner mental ones. They take the practice from behavior into the body, then to the edge of the mind:

  • Asana (3) - posture. In Patanjali it means a steady, comfortable seat for meditation, not the athletic postures we picture (you meet the full definition, Sutra 2.46, in Session 6).
  • Pranayama (4) - regulation and extension of the breath / life-force (prana). The breath is the lever that steadies the nervous system (full technique in Session 9).
  • Pratyahara (5) - withdrawal of the senses. Turning attention inward, away from the constant pull of sights and sounds - the hinge into the inner limbs.
Real world

You have felt pratyahara without naming it. Think of being so absorbed in a book that you did not hear someone call your name. The senses were still working - your attention simply turned inward. That involuntary flicker is a taste of the fifth limb; yoga just makes it something you can choose on purpose.

LiveThe inner limbs: dharana, dhyana, samadhi = samyama (6-8)2 min▶

The last three limbs are a single deepening arc of attention on one object, and together they have a name: samyama.

  • Dharana (6) - concentration. Holding the mind on a single point (a breath, a candle, a mantra). Attention keeps slipping; you keep bringing it back.
  • Dhyana (7) - meditation. When that focus becomes uninterrupted - a steady flow rather than repeated catching. Effort becomes ease.
  • Samadhi (8) - absorption. The sense of a separate "me observing" dissolves into the object of focus. This is the goal the whole ladder climbs toward.

The progression is one continuous zoom: you aim (dharana), the aim steadies (dhyana), the aimer disappears (samadhi). Trainings teach these three as theory; the deep techniques stay in personal practice and Session 9.

Part 3 · covers the ethical foundation of the path

The 5 yamas + 5 niyamas 5 min live

The first two limbs unpack into ten commitments - five outward-facing yamas and five inward-facing niyamas. These are the ethical spine of yoga and, in Session 3, the professional code you will teach by. Learn them as two lanes of five.

LiveAll ten, with plain meanings2.5 min▶

Sanskrit and a working translation for each. Learn the yamas as "how I treat others" and the niyamas as "how I treat myself":

SanskritPlain meaningGroup
AhimsaNon-harming - in action, speech and thoughtYama
SatyaTruthfulness - honesty that does not harmYama
AsteyaNon-stealing - including time, credit, energyYama
BrahmacharyaModeration / right use of vital energyYama
AparigrahaNon-possessiveness, non-greed, non-graspingYama
SauchaCleanliness and purity - of body and spaceNiyama
SantoshaContentment - peace with what isNiyama
TapasDiscipline, "self-heat", committed effortNiyama
SvadhyayaSelf-study - of texts and of oneselfNiyama
Ishvara PranidhanaSurrender to a higher power / the greater wholeNiyama
Flag: brahmacharya Classically, brahmacharya meant celibacy / chastity - it was written for renunciate monks. In most modern teacher trainings it is taught as moderation or "right use of your vital energy" - not squandering yourself. Both readings are legitimate; teach the range and name which lineage a given text is coming from, rather than asserting one meaning.
Real world

Aparigraha in a phone. Non-possessiveness sounds abstract until you look at a full camera roll or an overflowing inbox you cannot let go of. A student who "declutters" and feels lighter has practiced aparigraha without a mat in sight - proof these ten are life practices, not just yoga-class rules.

✦ Reflect nowRead back through the five yamas. Pick the ONE that your life is most quietly asking you to practice this week - the one that made you flinch a little. Write it down, and write one small, concrete way to practice it in the next seven days. (Example: aparigraha - I will delete one thing I have been clinging to.)
Self-studyWhy the yamas come before the poses1 min read▶

It is easy to skip straight to the mat. But Patanjali's order is deliberate: without ahimsa and satya, a "still mind" is just a well-stretched one. The ethics are not a prelude to yoga - they are the first two limbs of yoga. A teacher who lives the yamas is teaching them before they say a word, which is exactly the point of Session 3.

Part 4 · covers the sutras teachers quote

Sutras every teacher knows 6 min live

A handful of sutras come up again and again in trainings and in class. We recap the definition from Session 1, then add two working tools: the "four locks and keys" for keeping your mind steady around other people, and the five kleshas - the afflictions that drive most suffering, all rooted in one.

LiveSutra 1.2 - the definition, recapped1 min▶

From Session 1, the line the whole system rests on: Yogash chitta vritti nirodhah - "Yoga is the stilling of the fluctuations (vritti) of the mind (chitta)." Every limb you learned today exists to produce that one result. The eight limbs are the how; Sutra 1.2 is the what. Keep them paired in your memory.

LiveSutra 1.33 - the four locks & keys2 min▶

One of the most practical lines in the whole text. Patanjali says the mind stays clear and steady when you meet four kinds of people with four matching attitudes. Think of the people as "locks" and the attitudes as the "keys" that keep you calm:

When you meet the...Meet them with (key)
HappyMaitri - friendliness (not envy)
SufferingKaruna - compassion (not pity)
VirtuousMudita - joy / gladness (not resentment)
Non-virtuousUpeksha - equanimity (not judgment)
Real world

A teacher's reaction-management tool. A student brags about a handstand (happy) - answer with maitri, genuine gladness, not a flicker of "must be nice". Someone shows up in tears (suffering) - karuna, presence without fixing. A difficult, rude student (non-virtuous) - upeksha, keep your center, do not take the bait. Sutra 1.33 is, quite literally, how you keep your own mind steady while holding a room.

LiveSutra 2.3 - the five kleshas2 min▶

The kleshas are the five afflictions - the mental knots that cause suffering and keep the mind churning. The crucial insight: avidya (ignorance) is the root, and the other four grow out of it. Pull up ignorance and the rest lose their grip.

In order: avidya (ignorance, the root) → asmita (egoism / mistaking the ego for the true self) → raga (attachment to pleasure) → dvesha (aversion to pain) → abhinivesha (clinging to life, fear of death). Notice raga and dvesha are a pair: the same reflex, chasing what feels good and fleeing what feels bad.

Reflect & apply

Take the limbs off the page ✦ 8 min · everyone does this

Philosophy sticks when you run it in your own life. This week you will practice one ethic and use one tool - both small, both real. Do the first step now.

Practice one yama for a week. Take the yama you named in the Part 3 reflection. Each morning, set one concrete intention around it; each evening, note in a sentence how it went. Seven lines by next session.

Use the four keys on a real irritation. Think of one person who reliably gets under your skin. Which of the four locks are they - happy, suffering, virtuous, or non-virtuous? Name the matching key (maitri / karuna / mudita / upeksha) and try it, once, the next time you interact.

Spot your loudest klesha. Over a day, notice which affliction runs the show most - the ego (asmita), the chasing (raga), the avoiding (dvesha), or the low-grade fear (abhinivesha). Just naming it loosens it.

Keep this Your yama journal and your "loudest klesha" note feed straight into Session 3, where the yamas become a professional ethical code. Do not toss them.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Which limb is asana (posture) in Patanjali's eight-limbed order?

Asana is the third limb, after yama and niyama. The poses are one-eighth of the path, and not even the starting point.

2 · Which yama means non-harming?

Ahimsa is non-harming - in action, speech and thought. Satya is truthfulness; aparigraha is non-possessiveness.

3 · Which klesha is the root the other four grow from?

Avidya, ignorance, is the root klesha. Asmita, raga, dvesha and abhinivesha all grow out of it.
