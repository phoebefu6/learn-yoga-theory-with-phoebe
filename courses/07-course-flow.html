Course flow theory: sequencing a class

Anyone can call out a list of poses. Ordering them into a safe, intelligent arc that peaks and then lets a body down gently - that is the teacher's real craft. This session gives you the arc of a class, the peak-pose method, the sun salutations, and how flow changes by class type.

Part 0

Why this session exists

In Session 6 you learned what a posture is - the definition, the families, the alignment beneath it. But a class is not a pile of poses. Anyone can read a list of shapes off a card. The teacher's real craft is sequencing: arranging those poses into a safe, intelligent arc that warms the body, builds to something, and then brings it back down to rest. That craft is what "course flow theory" names - and it is where a knowledgeable practitioner becomes a teacher.

★ What you walk out with today The arc of a well-built class from centering to savasana, the mountain metaphor that shapes it, the peak-pose method with its two non-negotiable rules, the sun salutation A and B sequences, and a clear picture of how flow and pacing differ across the main class types.
Part 1 · covers the shape of a class

The arc of a class 5 min live

A well-built class has a shape - a rise and a fall. It opens quietly, warms the body, builds toward its most demanding moment, then eases back down into stillness. The classical image is a mountain: a gradual ascent, a summit, a descent, and finally rest at the base. Every class you ever teach will follow this arc, whatever the style.

LiveThe phases, in order2 min▶

The arc runs through seven phases, each preparing the next:

  • Centering - arriving, settling the breath, setting an intention. The base of the mountain.
  • Warm-up - gentle mobility, waking the joints and spine.
  • Sun salutations - flowing sequences that build heat and link breath to movement (Part 3).
  • Standing poses - the strong, activating middle of the climb.
  • Peak pose - the summit; the most demanding posture of the class (Part 2).
  • Cooling / floor work - seated poses, folds, gentle twists; the descent that releases what the peak built.
  • Savasana - final rest, integration. The body at the base again, transformed.
Real world

The class that forgot to come down. A new teacher builds a beautiful ascent to a big backbend - and then, running short on time, sends everyone straight to savasana. Students leave buzzing, wired, unable to settle. The mountain had a summit but no descent. The cooling phase is not optional filler; it is how you bring a stimulated nervous system back to earth. A class that only climbs is a class that abandons its students at altitude.

Part 2 · covers the method behind the arc

Vinyasa krama & the peak-pose method 6 min live

The arc tells you the shape; vinyasa krama tells you how to fill it intelligently. Vinyasa krama means an ordered, step-by-step progression - placing poses in a sequence where each one prepares the body for the next (the method is attributed to Krishnamacharya, whom you met in Session 1). The most common way to apply it is the peak-pose method: choose the single most demanding pose of the class, then make every other pose earn its place by preparing the body for that peak.

LiveHow to sequence around a peak2.5 min▶

Working backward from the peak is the whole trick:

  1. Identify the joint actions the peak demands - which joints open, in which direction (hip external rotation, shoulder flexion, spinal extension, and so on).
  2. Warm those areas with progressively deeper prep - a chain of poses that each open the needed joints a little more than the last.
  3. Peak - the body is now ready for the most demanding pose.
  4. Counterpose - immediately neutralize the deep pose (Session 6), returning the spine and joints toward neutral.
  5. Cool - descend through floor work into rest.
The two rules of the method Everything reduces to two: 1) warm the relevant joints before the peak, and 2) counterpose after a deep pose. If you remember nothing else about sequencing, remember these two - they keep bodies safe.
Real world

Building toward Camel. Your peak is Ustrasana (Camel), a deep backbend. Work backward: the pose needs open shoulders, open hip flexors, and spinal extension. So you prep with gentle chest-openers and shoulder work, lunges to open the hip flexors, and progressively deeper baby backbends - each step earning the next. After Camel, you counterpose with a gentle forward fold or a supine twist to release the low back, then cool to the floor. The peak is safe because the body arrived at it prepared.

Part 3 · covers Surya Namaskar A & B

Sun salutations A & B 5 min live

Sun salutations (Surya Namaskar) are the warming engine of most flowing classes - short, repeatable sequences that link one breath to each movement. Classically the breath is ujjayi (the soft ocean-sounding breath), and the governing rule is simple: one breath per movement, and if the breath cannot keep up, slow the movement down - never the reverse.

LiveSun A vs Sun B2.5 min▶

Sun Salutation A is the mobility warm-up - a wave that warms the spine and back body. It flows from Tadasana out to Down Dog and back:

Tadasana → Urdhva Hastasana (arms up) → Uttanasana (fold) → Ardha Uttanasana (half-lift) → Chaturanga → Up Dog → Down Dog (hold ~5 breaths) → Ardha Uttanasana → Uttanasana → Urdhva Hastasana → Tadasana.

Sun Salutation B is A plus strength and asymmetry. It adds two things:

  • Utkatasana (Chair) at the open and close - adding leg strength.
  • Virabhadrasana I (Warrior I) between Up Dog and Down Dog, on each side - adding unilateral leg work and deeper hip opening.

So Sun A warms and mobilizes; Sun B builds strength and works one leg at a time. Together they are the classic warming engine of a flow class.

Counts vary by school The exact pose count and breath count of a sun salutation differ between traditions and lineages - Ashtanga, Vinyasa, and Hatha schools each teach slight variations. Learn the sequence and its logic, not a single fixed number. If asked "how many poses," give the sequence and note the count is school-dependent.
Part 4 · covers class types & the energetic arc

Class types & the energetic arc 4 min live

The same seven-phase arc can be paced very differently depending on the class type. A vinyasa flow moves continuously; a yin class holds for minutes at a time. Underneath every style, though, runs the same energetic arc: a build in stimulation, then a settling - a movement from rajasic (active) energy toward sattvic (calm, balanced) energy, which is also a shift from the sympathetic nervous system to the parasympathetic (callbacks to the gunas in Session 4 and the nervous system in Session 5).

LiveThe four common class types2 min▶
TypePace & holds
Vinyasa flowDynamic, breath-linked, continuous movement; raises the heart rate
HathaSlower; postures held with controlled breath; more time in each shape
YinLong passive holds (2-20 min) targeting deep connective tissue; can feel intense
RestorativeFully prop-supported holds (5-20 min); pure relaxation, no effort asked

Breath-to-movement pacing is what separates them most. A flow class moves roughly one breath per movement, so the breath drives the tempo. A hatha class settles into a shape and breathes there. Yin and restorative barely move at all - the "flow" is the slow arc from one long hold to the next. Whatever the pace, the energetic arc holds: build first, settle second. You raise the sympathetic system through the active middle, then deliberately drop into the parasympathetic for the cool-down and savasana.

Reflect & apply · everyone does this

Sequence a class on paper ✦ 12 min · on paper

You will not teach a live class here - that is the in-person practicum - but you can design one on paper, and that is where sequencing is actually thought through. Two exercises.

Build around a peak. Choose a peak pose you know. Write down the joint actions it demands, then list three prep poses (progressively deeper) that warm those joints, and one counterpose to follow it. Check your two rules: warmed first, counterposed after.

Place the phases. Sketch the arc of a 60-minute class and place the seven phases along it - centering, warm-up, sun salutations, standing, peak, cooling/floor, savasana. Roughly how many minutes does each phase get?

Keep this This peak-based sequence is the skeleton of a real class plan. When you reach your in-person training and teach for the first time, this is exactly the worksheet you will fill in - you are already practising the core skill.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · In the peak-pose method, every other pose in the class exists to...

The peak is the most demanding pose; every other pose earns its place by warming the joints that peak needs. That is vinyasa krama.

2 · What should come right after a deep pose?

Rule two of the method: counterpose after a deep pose, to neutralize it and return the spine and joints toward neutral before cooling down.

3 · Sun Salutation B adds Warrior I and which other pose to Sun A?

Sun B = Sun A plus Chair (Utkatasana) at the open/close and Warrior I between Up Dog and Down Dog each side - adding strength and asymmetry.
