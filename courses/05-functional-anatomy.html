Part 0

Anatomy is a safety tool first

You do not need a physiotherapy degree to teach yoga. You need enough functional anatomy to answer one question in real time: is this student safe in this pose? Every idea in this session - planes of movement, the spine, the muscles that matter, the nervous system, the injury zones - exists to make you a teacher who can protect a room of different bodies. Learn it in that spirit and it stops feeling like a science exam and starts feeling like care.

Part 1 · covers planes, joints & the spine

Bones, joints, planes & the spine 6 min live

Two foundations. First, the three planes of movement - the directions a body can move, which let you describe any pose precisely. Then the spine, the structure you are protecting more than any other. A joint is simply where two bones meet; movement is what those joints do within a plane.

LivePlanes, joints and neutral spine2.5 min▶

Naming the plane makes cueing precise. A forward fold and a backbend are both sagittal - one is flexion (rounding forward), the other extension (arching back). A triangle pose lives in the frontal plane; a seated twist in the transverse. When you can name what a body is doing, you can teach it and, crucially, spot when it is doing too much.

  • Cervical (7 vertebrae) - the neck; most mobile and most vulnerable in shoulder and head stands.
  • Thoracic - the upper/mid back, anchored to the ribs; the region you want to open in backbends and rotate in twists.
  • Lumbar - the lower back; strong but the most common site of yoga complaints, so you protect it deliberately.

Neutral spine means the spine's natural curves are preserved - not flattened, not exaggerated. It is the reference position: you teach students to find neutral first, then move away from it into a fold or bend with control, then come back.

Self-studyWhy "stack the joints" is the master cue1 min read▶

Most alignment advice reduces to protecting joints by stacking them - shoulder over elbow over wrist, knee over ankle - so that bone carries load instead of soft tissue straining to hold a position. You will build the full alignment principles in Session 6 (asana theory). For now, hold the idea that anatomy in yoga is mostly about routing load through bones and away from vulnerable joints and ligaments.

Part 2 · covers the muscles that matter most in yoga

The muscles that matter in yoga 5 min live

You do not need every muscle in the body. A handful do most of the work in a yoga class - and understanding them explains why certain poses feel the way they do and where students tend to strain. The star of the show is a deep hip muscle almost nobody can feel directly: the psoas.

LiveThe four groups, and what they do2.5 min▶
Muscle groupRole in yoga
Psoas / iliopsoasThe strongest hip flexor and the only muscle tethering the legs to the spine (lumbar to pelvis); central to folds, lunges and backbends
HamstringsBack of the thigh; in yoga they are typically over-stretched and under-strengthened, a frequent source of strain in forward folds
Core (broad)Transverse abdominis, rectus, obliques, multifidus, pelvic floor and the psoas together; stabilises the spine and pelvis in nearly every pose
Spinal erectorsRun alongside the spine; extend it and hold you upright, doing much of the work in backbends and standing poses
Real world

Why a tight psoas turns backbends dangerous. Because the psoas connects the lumbar spine to the femur, a short, tight psoas pulls on the lower back the moment a student lifts into a backbend - compressing exactly the lumbar region you are trying to protect. That is why you warm the hip flexors before deep bends and cue length through the front of the hips, not just "lift the chest". One deep muscle explains a whole class of lower-back complaints.

Self-studyStrength and flexibility are partners1 min read▶

Yoga has a reputation as pure stretching, but the hamstrings tell the real story: constantly lengthened, rarely strengthened, they get long and weak, which is its own injury risk. A well-taught practice balances opening with engaging - the "effort and ease" principle you meet formally as sthira sukham asanam in Session 6. Anatomically, a joint is only as safe as the muscle that can both lengthen and support it.

Part 3 · covers why yoga calms the body

Why yoga calms: the nervous system 5 min live

This is the piece that turns "yoga makes me feel calm" into real physiology - and it is the single most useful thing you can understand as a teacher. The autonomic nervous system runs on two branches, and the breath is your direct lever on which one is in charge.

LiveTwo branches, one vagus nerve, one lever2.5 min▶

The autonomic nervous system (ANS) runs the body without your conscious control, on two opposing branches:

  • Sympathetic - "fight-or-flight": raises heart rate and alertness, speeds the breath, primes you for action. This is the rajasic state from Session 4.
  • Parasympathetic - "rest-and-digest": slows the heart, calms the breath, allows recovery and digestion. This is the sattvic settle.

The star is the vagus nerve - the longest parasympathetic nerve, carrying roughly 75% of parasympathetic outflow. It runs from the brainstem down through throat, lungs, heart, diaphragm and into the gut. And here is the mechanism that matters most for teaching: lengthening the exhale raises parasympathetic (vagal) tone. A longer out-breath is a direct, physical off-switch for the stress response.

Real world

The panicked student and the long exhale. Someone arrives wired - shallow, fast breathing, sympathetic branch running hot. You do not tell them to "relax". You guide a few breaths where the exhale is noticeably longer than the inhale. Within a minute the vagus responds, parasympathetic tone climbs, and the room can feel their shoulders drop. That is not mysticism - it is the exhale acting on the vagus nerve, the same rajas-to-sattva shift, made physiological.

Self-studyThe gunas, made physical1 min read▶

Notice how neatly this maps onto Session 4. The sympathetic branch is rajas - activity, heat, drive. The parasympathetic branch is sattva - clarity and calm. The energetic arc of a class you build in Session 7 (rajasic build, sattvic settle) is, in modern terms, deliberately moving students from sympathetic activation into parasympathetic recovery. Old map and new map describing the same body.

Part 4 · covers breathing anatomy + staying safe

Breathing anatomy & staying safe 5 min live

The breath is both the calm lever and, anatomically, a muscle you can teach. Then the payoff of the whole session: the five zones where yoga students get hurt, and one clear safety cue for each. Memorize these five - they are the heart of keeping a room safe.

LiveThe diaphragm and the three-part breath2 min▶

The diaphragm is the primary breathing muscle - a dome beneath the lungs that draws air in as it contracts, assisted by the intercostals between the ribs. Because the vagus nerve threads right through it, the diaphragm is also a physical regulator of the nervous system: how you breathe changes how you feel.

The classic teaching breath is the three-part breath (dirga): fill and empty in three moving parts - belly → ribs → chest. It teaches students to use the full diaphragm, slows the breath, and (with a lengthened exhale) reliably nudges the parasympathetic branch on. It is one of the calming breaths you will formalise in Session 9.

LiveThe five injury zones, with a cue each2.5 min▶
ZoneSafe-practice cue
WristsSpread the fingers to share the load; keep shoulders stacked over (not past) the wrists in weight-bearing poses
KneesKeep a micro-bend; in Warrior II the front knee stacks over the ankle and never collapses inward
Lower backBend the knees in forward folds and engage the lower abdominals; the most common yoga complaint
ShouldersIn Chaturanga the elbows hug the ribs and the shoulders stay above the elbows - no dumping forward
NeckSupport the neck in shoulder and head stands; never turn or force it under load

Injuries almost always trace to the same causes: overexertion, poor alignment, no warm-up, or attempting a pose before the body is ready. Your job is not to push students deeper - it is to keep them out of these five zones' danger and let depth come with time.

✦ Reflect nowWhich of these five zones is your own most tight or vulnerable area? Name the pose where you feel it, and the one safety cue above that would protect it. Teachers protect students best in the zones they have had to protect in themselves.
Reflect & apply 1 of 1

Feel the anatomy in your own body ✦ 8 min · everyone does this

Anatomy learned only on paper evaporates. This is the build-along of an anatomy session: find these three things in your own body now, so the words attach to sensation.

Find neutral spine. Sit or stand and locate the position where the spine's natural curves are preserved - not slumped, not over-arched. Round forward a little (flexion), arch back a little (extension), then return to neutral. That return is the skill you will teach most.

Prove the exhale rule. Take five breaths where the exhale is clearly longer than the inhale. Notice the shift - that is the vagus nerve raising parasympathetic tone in real time. This is your go-to tool for a stressed student or class.

Identify your at-risk joint. From the five injury zones, name the one that is tightest or most vulnerable for you, and the safety cue that protects it. Write it down; it will make you sharper at spotting the same risk in others.

Keep this Your neutral spine, your exhale experience, and your at-risk joint are three anchors you will lean on for the rest of the course - especially in asana theory (S6) and sequencing (S7), where all of this becomes how you build a safe class.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Flexion and extension - rounding forward and arching back - happen in which plane?

Both flexion and extension are forward-and-back movements, which live in the sagittal plane. Rotation is transverse; side-bending is frontal.

2 · Lengthening which part of the breath raises parasympathetic tone and calms the nervous system?

A longer exhale raises vagal (parasympathetic) tone - the direct physiological lever behind why yoga calms. It is your go-to for a stressed student.

3 · In Warrior II, the front knee should stack over the...

The front knee stacks over the ankle and never collapses inward - the key cue that keeps the knee safe in Warrior II.
