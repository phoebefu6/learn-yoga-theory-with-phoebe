Part 0

Why this session exists

Back in Session 2 you learned that asana - posture - is just one of the eight limbs of yoga. On paper it is a single spoke in a much larger wheel. In practice, it is the spoke almost everyone grabs first: asana is the doorway most students walk in through. That makes it the teacher's responsibility to understand the theory of posture - why a pose is built the way it is, what it does, and how to keep a body safe inside it - not just to demonstrate the shapes and hope.

★ What you walk out with today Patanjali's three-word definition of a posture and the balance-of-opposites principle beneath it, the seven families of asana and what each one does, the core alignment principles that keep any pose safe, and the ability to decode a Sanskrit pose name and pick the right prop for a restricted body.
One term, spelled one way The correct word is asana (Sanskrit asana, "seat" or "posture"). You will sometimes see "athana" written online - it is a phonetic misspelling with no basis in any yoga text. Say and spell asana; it is the word the tradition actually uses.
Part 1 · covers the definition of posture

Sthira sukham asanam 6 min live

Patanjali spends most of the Yoga Sutras on the mind - but he pauses to define posture in just three words (Sutra 2.46): sthira sukham asanam. A posture is sthira (steady, stable, grounded) and sukha (comfortable, easeful - literally "good space"). Not one or the other. Both, at once. That "and" is the whole philosophy of asana: a pose is a balance of opposing forces - effort and ease, strength and flexibility - and it is the reason a good teacher never forces a body into a shape.

LiveReading the three words2 min▶

Each word carries precision that the English loses, so hold all three:

  • Sthira - steady, stable, grounded. This is strength, not rigidity. A tree is sthira: rooted and firm, yet still able to move.
  • Sukha - literally "good space" (su + kha). Easeful, comfortable, unforced. The opposite is duhkha, "bad space" - suffering, the cramped feeling of straining.
  • Asanam - the posture / seat itself. The word originally meant the seated position for meditation before it came to mean posture in general.

Put together: a posture is the meeting point of stability and ease. Too much sthira and you are gripping and forcing; too much sukha and you are slumping with no integrity. The art of asana - and the art of teaching it - is finding the line where both are true.

Real world

Two students in the same pose. One grits her teeth in a forward fold, hamstrings screaming, chin jammed to shin - all sthira, no sukha; that is where injuries come from. The other bends her knees, lets the spine lengthen, and breathes - the same shape, but steady and comfortable. Sutra 2.46 tells you the second student is the one actually doing yoga. Your job as a teacher is to guide the first toward the second, never to push her deeper.

Self-studyWhy "never force" is a principle, not a nicety1.5 min read▶

The balance-of-opposites idea is not just gentle language - it is the philosophical basis for safe practice. If a pose must be both steady and comfortable, then the moment a body loses ease, it has left the definition of asana. Forcing past that point is not "going deeper"; it is stepping outside what the pose is meant to be. Most yoga injuries trace back to overexertion, poor alignment, or attempting a pose prematurely - all failures of sukha. When you cue "back off if the breath tightens," you are teaching Sutra 2.46, not being soft.

Part 2 · covers the families of posture

The families of posture 5 min live

There are hundreds of named postures, but they sort into a handful of families, and each family does a distinct job for the body and the energy. A teacher thinks in families, not in individual poses: a well-rounded class draws from several of them, because they move the body in complementary directions.

LiveWhat each family does2.5 min▶
FamilyWhat it does
StandingLower-body strength, balance, postural awareness; the most activating family
Seated / forward foldsPosterior-chain flexibility and hip mobility; calming; the base for pranayama
BackbendsEnergizing, heart- and chest-opening, spinal extension and flexibility
TwistsSpinal health and circulation (see the energetics note below)
InversionsCirculation, focus, clarity; traditionally said to refresh the whole system
Arm balancesUpper-body and core strength, concentration; usually peak-pose material
RestorativeRecovery and integration; deep parasympathetic calm, fully prop-supported
Where sources disagree: twist energetics Some traditions call twists energizing (they wring and stimulate the spine and organs); others call them calming and detoxifying. Both are taught. Don't present one as the single truth - say twists support spinal health and circulation, and note that their energetic effect is described differently across schools.
Self-studyWhy "well-rounded" matters1.5 min read▶

Families move the body in opposite directions, and the body wants that balance. A class that is all backbends over-works spinal extension; one that is all forward folds never opens the front body. The classical answer is to pair complementary directions - a backbend followed later by a fold, a twist to one side then the other - so the practice returns the body toward neutral. You will formalize this in Session 7 as counterposing and sequencing; for now, just notice that thinking in families is what lets you build a class that leaves a body more balanced than it started.

Part 3 · covers alignment principles

Alignment principles 5 min live

Alignment is not about hitting a picture-perfect shape - it is about arranging the body so that force travels safely and effort is shared. A handful of principles carry across almost every pose. The master idea: build from the foundation up. Whatever touches the ground is the base, and everything else stacks on top of it.

LiveThe principles, one at a time2 min▶
  • Build from the foundation up. Identify what touches the floor - feet, hands, sitting bones - and set it first. Everything above inherits that base.
  • Stack the joints. Line up ankle under knee under hip (or wrist under elbow under shoulder) so bone carries the load instead of soft tissue.
  • Distribute weight evenly. Spread the contact - all four corners of the foot, the whole palm - so no single joint takes the strain.
  • Engage the core and move from center. The center of gravity leads; the limbs follow. A stable center protects the low back.
  • Bandhas for energetic engagement. The energy locks you met in Session 4 - mula (root) and uddiyana (abdominal) - give a pose internal support and lift. They are alignment from the inside.
Real world

Fixing a wobbly Warrior from the ground. A student's front knee keeps caving inward. The temptation is to cue the knee - but the fault is usually below it. Cue the foundation: press the outer edge of the back foot down, spread the front foot's toes, and the knee tracks over the ankle on its own. You solved a knee problem at the floor, because the base was the real issue.

Part 4 · covers pose names, props & counterposes

Reading pose names + props & counterposes 5 min live

Sanskrit pose names look intimidating until you learn the pattern - and the pattern is remarkably consistent. Almost every name ends in -asana ("pose" or "seat"), and the rest of the name describes what the pose looks like: a position, a direction, an animal, an object. Learn to break a name into parts and you can decode poses you have never heard of.

LiveThe naming logic1.5 min▶

The pattern is usually direction / orientation + body part or object + -asana. Once you spot the ending, the rest reads like a description:

  • tada (mountain) + asana = Tadasana, mountain pose - standing tall and still.
  • bhujanga (cobra) + asana = Bhujangasana, cobra pose.
  • adho (down) + mukha (face) + svana (dog) + asana = downward-facing dog.

Names come from appearance, position, or inspiration - many are animals (dog, cobra, eagle), some are sages or objects. You do not need to memorize every root at once; you need the habit of breaking a name apart.

✦ Reflect nowDecode this one yourself: Tadasana. tada = mountain, asana = pose. So Tadasana = mountain pose. Now say what the pose probably looks like from the name alone - then check whether your guess matches.
Self-studyProps: bring the floor closer1.5 min read▶

Props are not a sign of weakness - they are alignment tools that make a pose accessible to the body in front of you. The governing rule is the one from Part 1: never force; honor current capacity. A prop's job is to bring the floor closer and enable correct alignment, so a restricted body (injury, stiffness, pregnancy) can keep practicing safely - never to yank someone deeper.

PropWhat it does
BlocksAdd height and stability; bring the floor up to the hand so alignment holds
StrapsExtend reach in a bind or fold - to keep the spine long, not to pull deeper
BolstersSupport the body in restorative poses so it can fully release
BlanketsLift the hips in seated work, cushion joints, add warmth in stillness
Self-studyCounterposes (pratikriyasana)1 min read▶

A counterpose (pratikriyasana) is a pose that neutralizes the one before it, returning the spine and joints toward neutral after a deep or intense posture. A gentle twist or fold after a strong backbend, a moment of stillness after an inversion - the counterpose releases the tension the deep pose created and keeps the body balanced. It is a small idea now; in Session 7 it becomes one of the two rules of sequencing.

Reflect & apply · everyone does this

Decode, balance, support ✦ 12 min · on paper

Theory sticks when you use it. Three short exercises, each one drawn from a part of today's session. Do them now, in a notebook or notes app.

Decode three names. Break these into parts and give the English: Tadasana, Bhujangasana, Adho Mukha Svanasana. Circle the -asana ending on each and write what the rest of the name describes.

Find sthira AND sukha. Pick one pose you already know. Write where the sthira (steadiness, effort) lives in it and where the sukha (ease) has to live for it to still be a posture. What would tip it out of balance?

Pick a prop. Imagine a student who cannot reach the floor in a standing forward fold. Which prop do you offer, and what does it do - remembering the rule is to bring the floor closer, never to force the fold deeper?

Keep this Your three decoded names are the start of your Sanskrit vocabulary. Add one new pose name every time you learn a pose, broken into parts - by Session 9 you will read most names on sight.
