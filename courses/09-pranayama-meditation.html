Part 1 · covers pranayama, the fourth limb

Pranayama: regulating the breath 5 min live

Pranayama is the fourth limb of Patanjali's eight - it sits right after asana, because a steady seat is what makes breath work possible. The word joins prana (the life-force we met in Session 4) with ayama (extension, regulation): the extension and regulation of the life-force through the breath. It is the bridge between the outer, physical limbs and the inner, meditative ones.

LiveThree techniques every teacher knows2.5 min▶

These three form the working core of a 200-hour pranayama syllabus. Each one you have already met a piece of earlier in the course:

  • Dirga (three-part breath) - the foundational breath from Session 5. You fill in three stages, belly then ribs then chest, and empty in reverse. It teaches full, diaphragmatic breathing and reliably calms the nervous system.
  • Ujjayi (ocean or victorious breath) - a gentle constriction at the throat makes a soft ocean-like sound. This is the breath used to pace a Vinyasa flow (Session 7): one breath, one movement.
  • Nadi shodhana (alternate-nostril breath) - breathing alternately through each nostril, said to balance the ida and pingala nadis from Session 4. Traditionally used to even out and steady the mind before meditation.

The bandhas from Session 4 - the energetic locks, especially jalandhara at the throat - support breath retention (kumbhaka) in more advanced pranayama, directing the breath and prana rather than letting it dissipate.

TechniqueEffect
Dirga (three-part)Full diaphragmatic breathing; grounding, calming, parasympathetic
Ujjayi (ocean)Paces movement to breath; steadies focus; warms the practice
Nadi shodhanaTraditionally said to balance the two nadis; evens and settles the mind
Teach gently, never strain Pranayama is powerful, and a strong breath practice can leave students light-headed or anxious if pushed. Never force retention or length; the breath should stay smooth and easeful. And frame the energetic claims honestly - nadi shodhana is traditionally said to balance the nadis; teach it as tradition, not as clinical fact.
Self-studyWhy the exhale calms you (a callback to S5)1.5 min read▶

Session 5 gave you the mechanism: the diaphragm is both the primary breathing muscle and a regulator of the autonomic nervous system, and lengthening the exhale increases parasympathetic (rest-and-digest) tone through the vagus nerve. That is the physiology under every "calming" pranayama. When you slow and extend the out-breath - in dirga, in a settling ujjayi, in nadi shodhana - you are not just relaxing psychologically; you are shifting the nervous system toward rest. It is the clearest place where the ancient practice and modern anatomy meet.

Part 2 · covers the inner limbs (5-8)

Meditation: the inner limbs 5 min live

The final four limbs are the inner journey of yoga - and they are the actual goal the whole system was built to reach. They move from turning attention inward, to gathering it, to sustaining it, to dissolving into it. This is where Session 1 comes full circle: the destination of these limbs is exactly the "still mind" of Sutra 1.2.

LivePratyahara → dharana → dhyana → samadhi2.5 min▶

The four inner limbs are a continuum, not four separate techniques - each one deepens into the next:

  • Pratyahara (limb 5) - withdrawal of the senses. Attention stops chasing sights and sounds and turns inward. This is the doorway between the outer limbs and meditation proper.
  • Dharana (limb 6) - concentration. Attention is gathered and held on a single point: the breath, a mantra, a sensation. Effortful, and repeatedly returning.
  • Dhyana (limb 7) - meditation. Concentration becomes an uninterrupted flow. You are no longer repeatedly bringing attention back; it simply stays.
  • Samadhi (limb 8) - absorption. The sense of a separate observer dissolves; only the object of meditation remains. This is union - the destination of the whole path.

When dharana, dhyana and samadhi happen together on a single object, the texts call it samyama - the integrated core of meditation you first met in Session 2. And notice where this lands: a mind gathered to a single point and absorbed is precisely chitta vritti nirodha, the "stilling of the fluctuations of the mind" from Sutra 1.2. The course ends where it began.

Real world

A breath-focus meditation you can lead. You do not need anything exotic to teach the inner limbs. At the close of a class: "Let your eyes close and your senses soften inward" - that is pratyahara. "Rest your full attention on the feeling of the breath at the tip of your nose; when the mind wanders, gently bring it back" - that is dharana. "Keep returning, and let the returns get shorter, until attention simply rests there" - you are pointing toward dhyana. Two or three quiet minutes of exactly this is real meditation instruction, delivered in plain language.

Self-studyMeditation is a skill you keep, not a state you force1 min read▶

Beginners often think they are "bad at meditation" because their mind wanders. But the wandering is the practice - dharana is defined by the returning. The skill you are building is not a blank mind; it is the gentle, patient act of coming back, again and again, without self-judgment. As a teacher, this is what you normalize for students: the point is not to stop the thoughts, it is to keep returning. That reframe alone keeps people meditating instead of quitting.

Part 3 · covers scope, ethics & professional development

Scope, ethics & professional development 4 min live

Now we step outside the philosophy. Being a good practitioner is not the same as being a responsible professional. A working teacher has duties the ancient texts never mention - knowing the limits of your role, keeping your skills current, protecting yourself and your students, and holding to the equity that modern yoga requires.

LiveKnowing the limits of your role2 min▶
  • Scope of practice. A yoga teacher is not a doctor, physiotherapist or psychotherapist. You teach yoga; you do not diagnose, treat or counsel. When a student presents a medical or psychological issue beyond a general practice, you refer them out to the right professional. Knowing this line is a mark of a good teacher, not a limitation.
  • Continuing education. Yoga Alliance registration expects ongoing CE hours - you keep learning after your 200 hours, not stop.
  • Insurance & liability. Working teachers carry liability insurance and understand basic waivers and duty of care. This is unglamorous and essential.
  • Ongoing self-practice (sadhana). You cannot pour from an empty cup or teach a practice you have abandoned. Your own steady practice is what keeps your teaching rooted and honest.
  • Equity in yoga. The 200-hour standard requires it (Session 3): accessible teaching, inclusive language, and genuine respect for yoga's cultural and philosophical origins. It is a professional duty, not an optional extra.
Not medical advice - refer out When in doubt, refer out. If a student describes pain, a diagnosed condition, pregnancy complications, or mental-health distress, your safest and most ethical move is to point them toward a qualified medical professional and stay within teaching general, well-cued yoga. "I'm a yoga teacher, not a doctor - please check with yours" is a complete and professional answer.
Part 4 · covers the path to certification

Your path to RYT-200 4 min live

This course taught you the theory - roughly the knowledge half of a 200-hour training, and genuinely most of what you will be examined on. But theory alone does not certify anyone to teach. Here, honestly, is what stands between where you are now and a Registered Yoga Teacher credential.

LiveWhat certification actually requires2 min▶

Certification is delivered by an accredited in-person Registered Yoga School (RYS). Beyond the theory you now hold, it adds the things only a live setting can give:

  • Contact hours. A minimum block of hours learning in person with faculty, not on a page.
  • Supervised practicum. Practising hands-on assists and adjustments on real bodies, with consent and live feedback (the ◐ items from Session 8).
  • Live teaching hours. Actually teaching a class, or parts of one, and being observed and corrected.
  • The school's assessment. Each RYS runs its own final evaluation and, on passing, you can register as an RYT-200.

Walk into that training already fluent in everything this course covered, and you will spend your in-person hours on what truly needs a room: your voice, your hands, and real students in front of you.

✦ Reflect nowPull out your notes from Session 1 - your "I want to teach yoga because..." and your own words for Sutra 1.2. Now draft a three-sentence teaching philosophy: (1) what yoga is to you, (2) what you want a student to leave your class with, (3) how you will teach - the tone and the values. This is the seed of who you will be as a teacher.
Self-studyWriting a teaching philosophy that is really yours1.5 min read▶

A teaching philosophy is not a marketing slogan - it is the compass you teach by. The strongest ones are specific and honest: they name the tradition you stand in, the tone you want in your room (invitational, from Session 8), and the one thing you most want students to feel. Draw it from your own practice, not from what sounds impressive. Revisit and rewrite it every year; as your practice deepens, so should the philosophy. When a hard teaching decision comes - how to handle an injury, whether to push a room - your philosophy is what you check against.

Reflect & apply 1 of 1

Breathe, sit, and write your philosophy ✦ 15 min · everyone does this

The course ends the way yoga does - not with more information, but with practice. Do all three, in order. The first two you can do right now, on your mat or in your chair.

Five rounds of three-part breath. Sitting tall, breathe in through the nose filling belly, then ribs, then chest; exhale slowly in reverse. Five full, unhurried rounds. Notice the settling - that is the parasympathetic shift from Session 5, happening in your own body.

Three minutes of breath-focus meditation. Soften your gaze or close your eyes (pratyahara), rest attention on the breath at the nostrils (dharana), and each time the mind wanders, gently return. Three minutes. The returning is the practice.

Write your teaching philosophy. Pull your Session 1 notes and finish the three-sentence draft from Part 4 - what yoga is to you, what a student should leave with, and how you will teach. This is the capstone of the whole course.

Keep this Your teaching philosophy is the single most useful thing to bring into an in-person training. Save it, date it, and rewrite it after your RYS - the distance between the two drafts will show you exactly how far you have travelled.
Homework

To close the course ◐ 30-45 min total

  • Be able to name three pranayama techniques and their effect: dirga (grounding, full breath), ujjayi (paces flow, steadies focus), nadi shodhana (traditionally balances the nadis, settles the mind).
  • Memorize the four inner limbs in order: pratyahara → dharana → dhyana → samadhi (withdrawal → concentration → meditation → absorption).
  • State the scope-of-practice rule in one sentence: a yoga teacher is not a doctor or therapist and refers medical or psychological issues out.
  • Decide your next concrete step toward an RYS - research one accredited in-person school you could realistically enroll in, and note what style it teaches.
  • Finish and date your teaching philosophy - the three sentences that say who you will be as a teacher.

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Pranayama is the regulation of...

Pranayama = prana (life-force) + ayama (extension/regulation). It is the fourth limb: the extension and regulation of the breath.

2 · What is the correct order of the last three limbs?

Concentration (dharana) deepens into meditation (dhyana) which dissolves into absorption (samadhi). Together they are samyama.

3 · A yoga teacher's scope of practice means...

A teacher teaches yoga; they do not diagnose, treat or counsel. Medical and psychological issues get referred to qualified professionals.
