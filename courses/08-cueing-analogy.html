Cueing & analogy theory: teaching craft

You can know a pose in your body and still fail to get it into someone else's. This session is about the language of teaching - the four ways you communicate a shape, why imagery beats anatomy for most students, how to invite instead of command, and how to touch only with consent.

Part 0

Knowing yoga and teaching it are two different skills

By now you understand the philosophy, the body, the postures and how to sequence them. But a room full of students does not learn from what is in your head - they learn from what comes out of your mouth, your hands, and your presence. Teaching is a craft of translation: taking a shape you feel and getting it into a body you cannot feel from the inside. This session is entirely about the language of teaching - how you use words, demonstration, touch and breath to move a shape from your body into someone else's.

Part 1 · covers the cueing channels + the 3-cue rule

The four cueing channels + the 3-cue rule 5 min live

You communicate a pose through four channels, not one. Good teachers move fluidly between them and, crucially, know when to stop talking. The most common beginner mistake is not too few cues - it is too many. Hence the 3-cue rule: roughly three cues per pose, then let the students actually be in the shape.

LiveThe four channels, and why more is not better2.5 min▶

Each channel reaches a different learner. Some students hear the shape, some need to see it, some only understand it once it happens in their own body, and some settle the moment you point them back to the breath.

  • Verbal cues - the spoken instruction: what to move, where to, and how. Your primary channel, and the one most prone to overload.
  • Demonstration - showing the shape yourself. The eye picks up in a second what a paragraph of words cannot convey.
  • Hands-on assists - a guiding touch that clarifies a direction of movement. Powerful, and governed entirely by consent (Part 4).
  • Pointing to the breath - "let the exhale carry you a little deeper". This is a cue too, and often the one that settles the whole room.

The 3-cue rule: give roughly three cues per pose, then stop. Beyond that, students stop hearing you and start bracing - they cannot process the shape and a stream of corrections at once. Cue, then let them inhabit it.

Real world

The teacher who drowns the room. A nervous new teacher pours ten cues into a single Warrior II: "front knee over ankle, back foot at 45, arms parallel to the floor, gaze past your fingertips, drop your shoulders, lengthen your tailbone, hug your front hip in, spread your toes, soften your face, breathe." By cue four nobody is listening; by cue ten the class is rigid and confused. Three cues - "front knee over ankle, reach through both arms, gaze past your front hand" - would have landed. The skill is choosing which three.

Self-studyWhich three cues? Choosing for the moment1.5 min read▶

The three cues are not fixed - they change with the room. A useful order: first the foundation (what is on the floor), then the main action (the one thing that makes the pose the pose), then a refinement or breath cue. Watch the actual bodies in front of you and cue what they need, not what your script says. If most of the room already has the foundation, skip it and spend your three cues elsewhere.

Part 2 · covers analogy, metaphor & imagery theory

Analogy & imagery theory 6 min live

Here is the most useful teaching idea in the whole session. For most students, an image lands better than an anatomical fact. "Externally rotate the femur" is precise and correct - and almost nobody can act on it. "Shine your inner thigh toward the back wall" produces the exact same movement, instantly. Understanding why this works turns you from someone who recites cues into someone who teaches.

LiveWhy imagery beats anatomy for most students3 min▶

The instinct of a new teacher is to share everything they learned in anatomy - the femur, the external rotation, the joint action. It feels rigorous. But for most students it does not work, and the reason is worth understanding:

  • The brain leans toward metaphor. We make sense of the abstract and unfamiliar by mapping it onto the concrete and familiar. That is how understanding works - not by receiving more facts.
  • The hard part is not receiving the detail - it is making sense of it. A student mid-pose is not short on information; they are short on a way to act on it. "External rotation" is a fact they now have to decode while balancing.
  • Metaphor lowers cognitive load. It maps the unfamiliar action onto something they already know deeply. No translation step, no decoding - the picture does the work.
  • Imagery directs them to the felt experience. A good image points the student at a sensation - something concrete to embody - rather than an idea to think about. "String pulling the crown of your head" is felt instantly.
  • Metaphors are actionable. Once the picture lands, we intuitively know what to do. "Grow roots from your feet" needs no further instruction; the body already understands rooting.

None of this means anatomy is useless - it is your foundation as a teacher, and it tells you which image to reach for. But between you and the student, the image is almost always the better bridge.

Anatomical cueThe analogy version
Externally rotate the femurShine your inner thigh to the back wall
Lengthen the spineA string is pulling the crown of your head up
Root down through the feetGrow roots from your feet into the floor
Broaden the collarbonesSmile across your chest, ear to ear
Engage the posterior chainPress the wall behind you away with your heel
Real world

The cue that finally landed. A teacher spent weeks telling a stiff-shouldered student to "depress and retract the scapulae" in Down Dog. Nothing changed - the student did not know what a scapula could do. One day she said instead, "melt your chest toward your thighs and let your shoulders slide down away from your ears". The shape transformed in a single breath. Same instruction, but one was a fact to decode and the other was a picture to feel.

✦ Reflect nowTake two dry anatomical cues you have heard in class - for example "posteriorly tilt the pelvis" and "abduct the shoulder blades". Convert each into an image a beginner could act on immediately, with no anatomy knowledge. Say each out loud. Which one made you feel the movement in your own body?
Self-studyBuilding your own image library1.5 min read▶

Great teachers collect images the way writers collect phrases. When a cue lands - when you see a room shift on a single sentence - write it down. Over time you build a personal library: the image for "root", the image for "lengthen", the image for "soften". The best images are drawn from things everyone already knows in their body - water, weight, string, warmth, reaching, melting - so no student is left decoding. Traditional yoga is rich with these ("roots like a tree", "reaching like branches"), and you are free to invent your own.

Part 3 · covers positive & invitational cueing

Invitational language 4 min live

There is a difference between a teacher who commands a room and one who invites it. Command language ("do this", "fix that") puts you above the student and their body under your authority. Invitational language ("you might explore...", "see if...", "notice...") keeps the student in charge of their own experience - which is not just kinder, it is more effective, because they stay present instead of bracing to obey.

LiveOffer options, not corrections2 min▶

Invitational language rests on a simple shift: you stop telling bodies what is wrong and start offering doorways. A "correction" implies the student failed; an "option" implies there is a next place to explore whenever they are ready. Both can point at the same movement - but one lands as judgment and the other as invitation.

Command phrasingInvitational rewrite
Straighten your front leg.See if your front leg wants to straighten a little more.
Drop your shoulders.You might let your shoulders soften down.
Go deeper into the fold.If it feels available, explore folding a little deeper.
Close your eyes.Close your eyes, or soften your gaze - whichever feels right.
Hold for five breaths.Stay for a few breaths, and come out whenever you need.
Adjustments guide, not force The same principle governs everything you do to a student's body, verbally or with your hands. An adjustment should guide toward a benefit, never force a shape. A well-taught student should be able to reach the same benefit on their own, without your touch - the cue and the option are what teach, not the correction.
Self-studyThe words that carry the invitation1 min read▶

A small set of phrases does most of the work: "you might...", "see if...", "notice...", "if it feels available...", "explore...", "whenever you are ready...", "or..." (the word that turns any instruction into an option). Sprinkle them and your whole tone changes without changing a single pose. The one caution: safety cues still need to be clear. "Keep the front knee over the ankle" protects the joint - say it directly. Invitational does not mean vague; it means non-coercive.

Part 4 · covers hands-on assists, consent & holding space

Hands-on assists & consent 4 min live

Touch is the most powerful cueing channel and the one that carries the most responsibility. A hands-on assist can clarify a shape a student has struggled with for months. It can also, without consent, cross a line that damages your entire relationship with a room. The modern, trauma-informed standard is simple and non-negotiable: explicit, enthusiastic consent, every time, and the power of choice always stays with the student.

LiveConsent is non-negotiable2.5 min▶

The rules are not complicated, but they are absolute:

  • Get explicit, enthusiastic consent before you touch anyone. Silence is not consent. A vague nod is not enthusiastic consent. If you are unsure, the answer is no.
  • Make it opt-in, and normalize declining. A student saying "no thanks" to an assist is completely normal and should never cost them anything - not your attention, not your warmth.
  • Students can change their mind mid-class. Consent given at the start of class can be withdrawn at any moment; a body that welcomed touch in one pose may not want it in the next.
  • Explain what and why before you touch. "I'm going to press gently on your sacrum to help you ground - is that okay?" tells the student exactly what is coming and lets them make a real choice.
  • The power of choice stays with the student. This is the trauma-informed heart of it. Your job is to offer, never to impose - the student is always the authority over their own body.
Real world

The consent card system. Many studios now place a small two-sided card or chip at the top of each mat before class. One side up means "yes, assists welcome today"; flip it and it means "no touch today, thanks". Students set it however they like as they arrive and can flip it any time. No one has to raise a hand, say no out loud, or explain themselves - the choice is silent, visible, and entirely theirs. It turns consent from an awkward moment into a normal part of setting up your space.

Self-studyHolding space: presence, voice and pacing1.5 min read▶

Beyond the specific channels, teaching is an act of holding space - creating a container calm and clear enough that students can turn inward safely. Three quiet tools do most of this work:

  • Observe. Spend more time watching the room than talking to it. Your cues should answer what you actually see, not run down a memorized script.
  • Voice. A steady, unhurried voice regulates a room the way a slow exhale regulates a nervous system. You set the pace of the room's breath with the pace of your speech.
  • Pacing. Leave silence. Students need unfilled moments to feel the pose and follow their breath. A teacher who never stops talking never lets the practice happen.

A teacher facilitates; they do not command. The best sign you are holding space well is that the room seems to forget you are there.

Reflect & apply 1 of 1

Rewrite a pose in teaching language ✦ 12 min · everyone does this

You learn to teach by writing cues, not by reading about them. Pick one pose you know well - Warrior II, Downward Dog, Triangle, anything. Now put every idea from this session onto it. Do it on paper or in a notes app.

Three anatomical cues into images. Write three dry, anatomical cues for your pose (e.g. "externally rotate the femur"), then rewrite each as an image a total beginner could act on. Read both versions out loud and feel the difference.

Cue the whole pose invitationally. Write your three chosen cues (remember the 3-cue rule) in fully invitational language - "you might...", "see if...", "notice...". No commands. Keep any real safety cue clear.

Script a one-line consent ask. Write a single sentence you would say before offering a hands-on assist in this pose - naming what you would do, why, and giving the student a genuine way to say no. Practice saying it warmly, not awkwardly.

Keep this This is the raw material of your teaching voice. Save it - in Session 9 you will fold cueing and consent into a short teaching philosophy, and these rewrites are its first evidence that you can actually teach, not just recite.
