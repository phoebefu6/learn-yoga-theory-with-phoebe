The Gita & the paths of yoga

Patanjali gave us a ladder; the Bhagavad Gita gives us a road map for a messy life. This session covers yoga's most beloved text, its three great paths, the art of acting without clinging to outcomes - and how the yamas become your professional ethics as a teacher.

Part 0

A battlefield, and a question

The Bhagavad Gita - "the Song of the Lord" - is a dialogue set on a battlefield, moments before a war, between a paralyzed warrior and his charioteer. It became yoga's most beloved and quoted text not because of the war, but because of the question underneath it: how do I act rightly when life is hard and the right choice is not clear? Where Patanjali's Sutras give a systematic ladder to a still mind, the Gita speaks to the part of you that has to get up and do something in a difficult world. That is why teachers reach for it constantly.

Part 1 · covers the setting of the Bhagavad Gita

The story behind the teaching 4 min live

You do not need to know the whole epic - you need the scene. It sets up everything the text teaches, and it doubles as a metaphor you will use with students for years.

LiveThe scene, in a paragraph2 min▶

On the eve of a great battle, the warrior Arjuna looks across the field and sees relatives, teachers and friends on the other side. Overwhelmed, he drops his bow: he cannot bring himself to fight. His charioteer is Krishna - who is revealed to be the divine in human form - and what follows is their dialogue. The Gita is 700 verses long and sits inside a much larger epic, the Mahabharata. Krishna does not tell Arjuna to walk away; he teaches him how to act rightly, from a steady centre, in a situation with no clean answer.

The battlefield, Kurukshetra, is read as a metaphor: the "field of action" is your own life, and the war is the daily struggle to do the right thing. Arjuna is you at your most stuck; Krishna is the wiser voice that steadies you.

Real world

Every student is a little bit Arjuna. Someone who freezes at the edge of a big life decision - leave the job, have the hard conversation, start the training - is standing in their own Kurukshetra. When you frame a practice as "steadying yourself so you can act", you are teaching the Gita, whether or not you ever name it in class.

LiveDharma - the idea underneath it all2 min▶

The word the Gita turns on is dharma - your duty, your right action, the role that is genuinely yours to play. Arjuna's crisis is a dharma crisis: what is the right thing for me, specifically, to do here? Krishna's answer is not "follow a rule" but "act in line with your true duty, wholeheartedly, and let go of the rest".

Dharma is deliberately personal. A teacher's dharma is not a student's; a parent's is not a monk's. Part of maturing - as a person and as a teacher - is discerning what is genuinely yours to do and doing it well, rather than living someone else's life. Hold onto this word; it returns in Part 3 as the root of professional ethics.

Part 2 · covers the three paths of yoga in the Gita

Three paths up one mountain 6 min live

The Gita's great gift to yoga is this: there is not one way up. It names three paths - suited to different temperaments - that all reach the same summit. A doer, a lover, and a thinker each have a road home. Knowing which is yours (and your students') shapes how you practice and teach.

LiveKarma yoga - the path of action1.5 min▶

Karma yoga is the yoga of selfless action: doing your work in the world wholeheartedly, as an offering, without grasping at what you get back. Its signature phrase is nishkama karma - action free of craving for the fruits (Part 3). This is the path for the doer, the person who finds the sacred through service and effort rather than sitting still.

LiveBhakti yoga - the path of devotion1.5 min▶

Bhakti yoga is the yoga of loving devotion: reaching the summit through the heart - love, surrender, and relationship with the divine (however you understand it). Chanting, kirtan and heartfelt practice live here. It is the path for the lover, the one who is moved more by feeling than by logic or labour.

LiveJnana yoga - the path of knowledge1.5 min▶

Jnana yoga is the yoga of knowledge and inquiry: reaching the summit through study, discernment and the relentless question "who am I, really?" It is the path for the thinker, the one who cuts through illusion with clear seeing. Patanjali's svadhyaya (self-study) lives close to this road.

Flag: the "fourth path" You will often hear of a fourth path - Raja yoga, the "royal path" of meditation, associated with Patanjali. It is a beautiful framing, but it is a later synthesis (popularized by Swami Vivekananda in the 19th-20th century), not one of the Gita's own three. Teach karma / bhakti / jnana as the Gita's paths, and mention Raja as a later addition - not as if the Gita named four.
Part 3 · covers the Gita's most practical teaching

Action without attachment 5 min live

If you remember one idea from the Gita, make it this. Nishkama karma: act fully, do your part with everything you have - and release your attachment to the results. You have a right to the action, never to its fruits. It sounds passive; it is the opposite.

LiveWhy letting go makes you act better, not worse2 min▶

The fear is that if you stop caring about results you will stop trying. The Gita says the reverse. When your peace is hostage to the outcome, you act anxiously - hedging, grasping, half-present. When you pour yourself fully into the action and hold the result lightly, you actually show up more completely, because there is no fear of the fruit contaminating the work. Nishkama karma is not indifference; it is wholehearted effort minus the anxious clinging.

Real world

Your first class as a teacher. You can prepare the sequence, arrive early, cue with care - all yours. Whether eight people come or two, whether they love it, whether anyone rebooks - not yours. Teach the class you meant to teach and release the attendance sheet, and you will teach a far better class than the version of you white-knuckling the outcome.

✦ Reflect nowName one thing in your life right now where you are gripping the OUTCOME - a result you cannot fully control but keep clutching. Separate it into two columns: what is genuinely yours to give (the effort), and what is not yours to own (the result). Write one sentence on how you would act differently if you fully released the second column.
Part 4 · covers yoga ethics for teachers

Yoga ethics for teachers 6 min live

The philosophy now becomes professional. As a teacher you hold real influence over people who arrive vulnerable. The yamas from Session 2 become a working code of conduct, and a few modern commitments sit alongside them. This is a preview; consent and trauma-informed teaching get their full session in Session 8.

LiveThe yamas as a professional code2 min▶

The five yamas are not just personal practice - for a teacher they are a code of conduct:

  • Ahimsa (non-harming) - never push a student into pain; language and adjustments that protect, not perform.
  • Satya (truthfulness) - do not overclaim benefits or credentials; be honest about what you know and do not know.
  • Asteya (non-stealing) - credit your teachers and sources; do not pass off others' sequences or words as your own.
  • Brahmacharya (right use of energy) - clear professional boundaries with students; no exploiting the trust of the room.
  • Aparigraha (non-grasping) - do not cling to students, status, or a full class at the cost of your integrity.

This is dharma from Part 1 made concrete: your duty as a teacher, lived out in how you conduct yourself.

LiveScope, equity, and consent2 min▶
  • Scope of practice. You teach yoga. You are not, in that role, a physiotherapist, psychotherapist or doctor. Knowing the edge of your competence - and referring out past it - is itself an ethical act.
  • Equity in yoga. The Yoga Alliance requires an equity component in every training. It means teaching for accessibility (bodies, budgets, backgrounds), holding cultural respect for yoga's Indian roots, and not appropriating or hollowing out the tradition you profit from.
  • Consent (preview). Students have the right to say no - to a hands-on adjustment, to a practice, to being touched at all. You will build the full consent and trauma-informed toolkit in Session 8; for now, hold it as a non-negotiable.
Not medical or therapeutic advice Nothing in this course - or in a 200-hour training - qualifies you to diagnose, treat or give medical or mental-health advice. When a student's needs exceed general yoga teaching, refer them to a qualified professional. Staying in scope protects them and you.
Real world

The student with a bad back. Someone says "my doctor mentioned a disc thing - what poses will fix it?" The ethical answer is not a sequence. It is satya plus scope: "I can offer gentle, optional movement and you skip anything that hurts, but for the disc itself please work with your doctor or a physio." That sentence is your ethics in action.

Reflect & apply

Find your path, loosen your grip ✦ 8 min · everyone does this

Two practices this week: name your dominant path, and put non-attachment to work on something real. Start now.

Identify your dominant path. Doer, lover, or thinker - which of karma (action), bhakti (devotion), or jnana (knowledge) most describes how you naturally reach for the sacred? There is no wrong answer, and most people blend two. Name your lead path and one line on why.

Practice non-attachment on one outcome. Take the gripped outcome from the Part 3 reflection. For one week, put your full care into the effort and consciously release the result each time you catch yourself clutching. Note what changes in how it feels to act.

Draft your dharma. Finish the sentence: "As a future teacher, my duty (dharma) is to..." Keep it to one honest line. You will build on it in Session 9's teaching philosophy.

Keep this Your dominant path, your dharma line, and one equity commitment (below) are all first drafts of the teaching philosophy you write in Session 9. Save the page.
Homework

Before Session 4 ◐ 30-45 min total

  • Name the three paths with a one-line meaning each: karma (selfless action), bhakti (loving devotion), jnana (knowledge / inquiry).
  • Be able to state the Raja caveat: Raja yoga is a commonly-added fourth "royal path" of meditation, but it is a later synthesis, not one of the Gita's own three.
  • Write your dharma as a future teacher in one to two sentences.
  • Write one concrete equity commitment - a specific way you will keep your teaching accessible and culturally respectful.
  • Optional deeper read: find a translation of Bhagavad Gita 2.47 (the classic verse on action without attachment to its fruits) and sit with it.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · In the Bhagavad Gita, who counsels the warrior Arjuna?

Krishna, Arjuna's charioteer and the divine in human form, delivers the teaching. Patanjali wrote the Yoga Sutras; Svatmarama the Hatha Yoga Pradipika.

2 · Karma yoga is the path of...

Karma yoga is selfless action. Bhakti is loving devotion; jnana is the path of knowledge and inquiry.

3 · Nishkama karma means to...

Nishkama karma is wholehearted action released from clinging to its fruits - full effort, open hands. Not passivity, and not acting for the reward.
