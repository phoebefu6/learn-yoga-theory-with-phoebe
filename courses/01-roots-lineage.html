Why this session exists

Most people meet yoga through the poses and assume that IS yoga. In a 200-hour teacher training, the poses are one of eight limbs, and the history behind them is a graded exam topic. This course teaches the theory half of a 200-hour certification - the philosophy, anatomy, asana logic, sequencing, and teaching craft you are expected to know - distilled into nine focused sessions. Today lays the foundation: what the word means, where it came from, and how the pieces fit together.

Part 1 · covers "what is yoga" + the goal of practice

What yoga actually is 6 min live

The word yoga comes from the Sanskrit root yuj - to yoke, to join, to unite. Not "stretching". Union: of body and breath, of individual awareness and something larger, and above all a mind that has stopped fighting itself. Patanjali, the sage who systematized yoga, gave the whole thing a one-line definition we will keep returning to.

LiveThe one-line definition: Yoga Sutra 1.22 min▶

The most quoted line in all of yoga is Patanjali's second sutra: Yogash chitta vritti nirodhah - "Yoga is the stilling of the fluctuations (vritti) of the mind (chitta)." Everything else - the poses, the breathing, the ethics - exists to produce that one result: a mind that is clear and settled rather than churning.

  • Chitta is the whole field of mind-stuff: thoughts, memories, impressions, the running commentary.
  • Vritti are the "whirlpools" - the turnings and fluctuations that keep it stirred up.
  • Nirodha is the settling, the stilling. Not forcing the mind blank - letting the water go clear.
✦ Reflect nowWrite down, in one honest sentence, what you thought "yoga" meant before today. Now write Patanjali's definition next to it: "the stilling of the fluctuations of the mind." What changes about why you would practice?
Real world

Why students actually keep coming back. Ask a room of long-term practitioners why they practice and almost nobody says "flexibility". They say "it's the one hour my head goes quiet". That is Sutra 1.2 in plain language - they are describing chitta vritti nirodha without knowing the Sanskrit. The poses were the doorway; the still mind is why they stayed.

Self-studyThe four aims and why yoga is not just exercise2 min read▶

Classical Indian thought frames a full human life around four aims (purusharthas): dharma (duty, right living), artha (prosperity, means), kama (pleasure, desire) and moksha (liberation, freedom). Yoga is traditionally a path toward the last one - freedom from the suffering caused by an unsteady, grasping mind. That is why the texts treat it as a complete philosophy of living, not a fitness regimen.

  • Modern postural yoga - what most Western studios teach - is a recent branch of a very old tree. Knowing the tree is what separates a teacher from an instructor.
  • You do not have to be religious to teach or practice yoga. But you are expected to know the tradition you are standing in, and to represent it honestly. That respect is itself part of the ethics (Session 3).
Self-studyA note on Sanskrit (and why we keep it)1 min read▶

Yoga's technical vocabulary is Sanskrit, and teacher trainings expect you to know the key terms. We will always give the Sanskrit and a plain-English meaning, because the words carry precision that translation loses - sukha is not quite "comfort", prana is not quite "breath". Learn the terms the way you would any professional vocabulary: a few at a time, in context. By Session 9 they will feel like your own.

Part 2 · covers yoga history & the classical texts

The timeline: from the Vedas to your studio 6 min live

Yoga did not arrive fully formed. It grew across roughly three thousand years, in layers, each adding something the next built on. You do not need every date - you need the order and what each layer contributed. Dates here are scholarly estimates with wide ranges, so hold them loosely.

LiveThe classical texts, in one pass2.5 min▶

Four texts do most of the work in a 200-hour syllabus. You meet each properly in a later session - here is the map so the names are familiar:

TextWhat it gave yogaSession
The Vedas & UpanishadsThe philosophical ground: the self, consciousness, and the five koshas (sheaths of being) from the Taittiriya UpanishadS4
Bhagavad GitaThe paths of yoga - action (karma), devotion (bhakti), knowledge (jnana) - and acting without attachment to resultsS3
Patanjali's Yoga SutrasThe systematic core: the definition of yoga and the eight limbs (ashtanga) that structure the whole pathS2
Hatha Yoga PradipikaThe physical practices - postures, breath control, energy locks - and the subtle-body system behind themS4, S6
Exam-honest note The total number of sutras is often cited as either 195 or 196, and the Yoga Sutras are dated anywhere from 100 BCE to 500 CE. Trainings differ. If asked, give the range and note ~400 CE as the common estimate rather than a single "correct" year - that is what a well-read teacher does.
Self-studySix schools, and where yoga sits2 min read▶

Classical Indian philosophy has six traditional "schools" (darshanas). Yoga is one of them, closely paired with another called Samkhya, which supplies its underlying model of reality (spirit and matter, and the three gunas or qualities of nature - you meet the gunas in Session 4). You will not be tested on all six, but knowing yoga is a recognized philosophical system, not a folk practice, changes how seriously you carry it.

The practical takeaway: when a text or teacher references Samkhya, they are pointing at yoga's philosophical parent - the source of its map of mind and matter.

Part 3 · covers modern yoga history & styles

The modern lineage: one teacher, many styles 5 min live

Here is the surprise that reframes everything: most of the yoga styles in the world today trace back to a single 20th-century teacher and a handful of his students. When you understand this lineage, the confusing menu of studio styles suddenly makes sense.

LiveKrishnamacharya, "the father of modern yoga"2 min▶

Tirumalai Krishnamacharya (1888-1989) taught at the Mysore Palace in southern India and, more than anyone, shaped the physical yoga practiced worldwide today. His genius was adaptation - "teach what is appropriate for the individual" - and his students carried different facets of his teaching in different directions:

  • B.K.S. Iyengar (his brother-in-law) emphasized precise alignment and the use of props so any body could find a pose safely - the root of the careful, detailed instruction style.
  • K. Pattabhi Jois systematized Ashtanga Vinyasa: fixed, vigorous sequences linked to breath. This is the direct parent of modern Vinyasa Flow and Power Yoga.
  • T.K.V. Desikachar (his son) developed Viniyoga: yoga adapted to the individual's needs and body, the therapeutic thread.
Real world

Why your local schedule makes sense now. The studio that lists "Vinyasa Flow", "Power", "Iyengar" and "Gentle/Therapeutic" side by side is really listing Krishnamacharya's students. Same grandparent, different emphases. When a new student asks "which class should I take?", you are really asking which branch of this one lineage fits them.

Self-studyA field guide to the common styles2 min read▶

You do not need to teach all of these - but a teacher should be able to describe them:

StyleFeel
HathaUmbrella term for physical yoga; in class listings, usually slower, held postures with breath
Vinyasa / FlowContinuous, breath-linked movement; sequences vary each class (Session 7)
AshtangaThe same vigorous set sequence every time; the source of Vinyasa
IyengarPrecise alignment, long holds, props; deeply detailed
YinLong passive holds (2-20 min) targeting deep connective tissue; can be intense
RestorativeFully prop-supported, near-stillness; pure recovery and calm
Yin vs Restorative These get confused constantly. Yin loads connective tissue with long holds and can feel intense; Restorative supports the body completely and asks for no effort at all. Knowing the difference is a quick way to sound like you know your craft.
Part 4 · covers the RYS-200 knowledge areas

The map: four knowledge areas, nine sessions 4 min live

The Yoga Alliance - the main global registry for yoga schools and teachers - defines a 200-hour training as four core knowledge areas. Every session in this course lives inside one of them. Here is the whole map, so you always know where you are.

Notice the shape: we start in the mind and philosophy (Sessions 1-3), move into the body both subtle and physical (4-5), then to the postures and how to string them together (6-7), and finish with the craft of teaching (8-9). Philosophy first, poses later - the same order history took.

Self-studyWhat "Yoga Alliance" and "RYT-200" actually mean1 min read▶

Yoga Alliance is a voluntary registry, not a government license. A school that meets its standards is an RYS (Registered Yoga School); a graduate who registers becomes an RYT-200 (Registered Yoga Teacher, 200-hour). Registration signals your training met a recognized minimum - many studios ask for it - but it is a credential, not a legal requirement to teach. Standards were updated in 2020 (the "Elevated Standards"), which is why current trainings emphasize the four defined areas plus a required equity-in-yoga component.

Reflect & apply 1 of 2

Place yourself on the map ✦ 8 min · everyone does this

Theory sticks when you locate yourself inside it. This is the "build-along" of a theory course: not a pose, but your own orientation. Do it now, on paper or in a notes app.

Your doorway. Which style did you first practice, and which teacher/lineage does it descend from (use the Part 3 lineage)? Write the chain: your style → its founder → Krishnamacharya.

Your layer. On the Part 2 timeline, which text are you most curious about - the Gita's paths, Patanjali's limbs, or the Hatha subtle body? That curiosity is your strongest study thread; note it.

Your why. Rewrite Sutra 1.2 in your own words, then finish the sentence "I want to teach yoga because..." - and check whether your "why" is about the poses or about the still mind. Both are valid; knowing which is yours matters.

Reflect & apply 2 of 2

Explain yoga to a skeptic ✦ 6 min · pair up if you can

The fastest test of understanding is teaching it. Imagine a friend who thinks yoga is "just stretching for flexible people". In three sentences, no jargon, correct them - using union, the still mind, and the fact that poses are one small part.

✦ Try this framing"Yoga actually means 'to unite' - it's a 2,000-year-old system for settling the mind, and the stretching part is one of eight tools it uses. The famous definition is literally 'yoga is the stilling of the fluctuations of the mind.' So the flexibility is a nice side effect - the real goal is the hour where your head finally goes quiet."

Say it out loud once from the card, then once in your own words without looking.

If you have a partner, have them play the skeptic and push back ("so it's still just exercise?"). Answer with the eight-limbs point - you will go deep on those next session.

Homework

Before Session 2 ◐ 30-45 min total

  • Memorize the timeline order (Vedas → Upanishads → Gita → Yoga Sutras → Hatha texts → modern postural yoga). Not the dates - the order and one contribution each.
  • Memorize the lineage chain: Krishnamacharya → Iyengar / Pattabhi Jois / Desikachar → the styles each founded.

  • Look up which style(s) your future training school teaches, and trace it back to the lineage. Notice what it emphasizes (alignment? flow? adaptation?).

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · The Sanskrit root yuj, which gives us the word "yoga", means...

Yoga means union - of body, breath and mind. The poses are one doorway in, not the whole house.

Vedas (~1500 BCE) → Yoga Sutras (~400 CE) → Hatha Yoga Pradipika (~15th c CE). Philosophy came long before the postural texts.
